There's a question that doesn't get asked enough: why are some countries poor?

The usual answer blames something inside those countries: corruption, culture, bad governance. That answer is comfortable because it lets the rest of the world off the hook.

This guide makes a different case: most of the world's poorest countries aren't poor because wealth never existed there. They're poor because it was taken. Colonialism. Debt. Coups. Trade rules written by the winners. The extraction didn't stop when the flags came down. It just changed its name.

Follow the money.

Station 1 of 5

There Are No Poor Nations

Roughly $150 trillion has been extracted from the Global South since colonialism began, and more leaves every year than arrives. Africa is a net lender to the rich world. Poverty there is not a starting condition anyone failed to escape. It is a running total.

Not as metaphor: the wealth was extracted over 300 years of colonialism, and it is still leaving today through debt repayments, profit transfers, and rigged trade rules.

"Africa is not poor. Africa is being looted."

One correction before we go further. "Rich nations took from poor nations" is a useful first approximation and a bad final answer. Nations don't own things; classes within them do. The wealth did not arrive in Britain and get shared out among Britons, and it did not leave Nigeria without Nigerians handling it on the way. Both ends of that sentence have a class structure, and the two dives below are about who is actually standing at each end. This matters because it decides what any of it means for you.
Deep Dive: The Partners

Extraction Always Had Local Shareholders

No empire has ever had the manpower to hold a continent by force alone. Every extraction system in history ran on a local class that did well out of it, and pretending otherwise makes the last sixty years impossible to explain.

The story where colonisers arrive, take everything, and leave a purely victimised population behind is emotionally clean and historically false. British India was administered by a few thousand British officials over hundreds of millions of people. That is not a ratio you achieve with soldiers. You achieve it with local princes, landlords, tax farmers, merchants and soldiers who were paid, promoted and protected for their part in it.

Marxists call this the comprador class: the ownership class inside a poorer country whose own wealth depends on servicing extraction by a richer one. The traditional name is "comprador bourgeoisie" ("comprador" is borrowed from Portuguese traders in Macau and means, roughly, "buyer"). Not puppets, which implies no agency. Not victims, which implies no benefit. Partners, with interests of their own that happen to align.

This is the half of Walter Rodney that usually gets cut. This guide closes with his line about nations whose wealth was stolen, and that line is true. But Rodney was a Marxist, not a nationalist, and How Europe Underdeveloped Africa is explicit that African ruling groups participated in and profited from the trade in human beings and from colonial administration. He was killed in 1980 by a bomb in Guyana, at the hands of a government led by Africans, while organising against it. He was not confused about whether an African government could be an enemy of African workers.

The classic statement is Frantz Fanon's, in the chapter of The Wretched of the Earth (1961) called "The Pitfalls of National Consciousness". Fanon predicted, while the independence flags were still being sewn, that the new national ownership class of the newly free colonies would not build anything. It would inherit the colonial apparatus, keep the extraction running, take a commission on it, and use nationalism to keep its own workers quiet. He was describing 1960. He was also describing 1985, and 2026.

What this explains that "rich nations vs poor nations" cannot

Why decolonisation produced new local ownership classes instead of socialism. In most cases the colonial state was not dismantled at independence. It changed personnel. The mines, ports, plantations and marketing boards kept operating, and the people now running them had every reason to keep the arrangement rather than break it.

Why some of the world's richest people live in some of the world's poorest countries. Nigerian oil wealth, Gulf petro-monarchies and Indian billionaires are not anomalies in the extraction story. They are positions in it. A framework that only counts wealth crossing borders cannot see them at all.

Why the coups in Station 2's deep dive targeted who they targeted. Look at the list again: Mosaddegh in Iran, Arbenz in Guatemala, Lumumba in Congo, Allende in Chile, and Thomas Sankara, Burkina Faso's revolutionary president, who canceled his country's debt repayments and championed African self-reliance before he was overthrown and killed in a 1987 coup led by his former ally Blaise Compaoré. What each of them threatened was not "the West" in the abstract. It was a specific arrangement between foreign capital and a domestic class that was doing well out of the status quo, which is why in almost every case the coup had enthusiastic local partners. Somebody at home always opened the door.

Why aid and reparations are harder than they look. A transfer between states is a transfer to whoever controls that state. If the extraction ran partly through a domestic ownership class, then money handed to that class is not obviously a repair. This is not an argument against reparations. It is the reason the question "who receives" is a serious one rather than a stalling tactic, and it is why the reparations claim brought by Caricom, the political and economic union of fifteen Caribbean nations, is framed around development programmes, health systems and debt cancellation rather than a cheque.

What this is not saying. It is not "they did it to themselves," which is the oldest excuse in the file and the exact move Station 4 is about. The initiative, the guns, the capital and the overwhelming majority of the proceeds were foreign. Local partners took a commission on a business someone else was running. Naming them adds a class to the picture; it does not move the responsibility.
Deep Dive: Your Position

Why the Workers of Rich Countries Rarely Opposed Any of This

You are not standing outside this looking in. If you live in a wealthy country, some part of what you consider a normal standard of living is the far end of the pipe described in Station 1, and that is the single most uncomfortable sentence in this guide.

Here is a question the "rich nations robbed poor nations" framing cannot answer. Over 300 years of colonialism, and 70 more of debt and coups, the working class of Britain, France and the United States was for the most part not opposed. Sometimes it was actively enthusiastic. If empire only enriched a handful of owners, why did the people who owned nothing keep voting for it, enlisting for it, and cheering it?

Lenin's answer, in Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1916), is the piece of his argument that popular explainers usually leave out, including the earlier version of this guide. Empire generates superprofits, returns far above what is available at home. A portion of those returns gets spent domestically: on higher wages in some sectors, on concessions to organised labour, and eventually on pensions, housing and health services. The effect is to create what he called a labour aristocracy, a layer of workers in the imperial core whose conditions are cushioned by the arrangement and who therefore have something real to lose if it ends.

He was blunt about the consequence: this is why the socialist parties of Europe voted for war credits in 1914 instead of the general strike they had promised. Their base had been partly bought, with money taken somewhere else.

The part that makes this hard to see is that nobody ever hands you anything. There is no cheque with "empire" written on it, and your employer did not raise your pay because of a coup somewhere. The transfer arrives as prices, not as income. When the cobalt, the coffee, the cotton and the garment labour at the far end of the chain are cheap, your wage buys more without anyone increasing it. Your real standard of living rises and your payslip never moves. That is the whole mechanism, and it is invisible precisely because it operates on the side of the ledger nobody examines.

What that looks like in your actual life. The price of a phone containing Congolese cobalt dug by people earning a few dollars a day. Clothes priced by wages a structural-adjustment programme drove down. A pension fund that holds the arms manufacturers in Part 5 and the extractive miners in this one, and pays out to you when they perform well. A currency strong enough to import food cheaply because it is the one debts are denominated in. Public services in Britain and France built, in part, during the period the drain in Station 1 was at its peak.

Which is why "stop shopping at the wrong companies" was never going to be the answer to any of this. If you live in a wealthy country, you're in this picture, in a specific position within it: not an owner, not a beneficiary in the way the owners are, but not a neutral bystander either.

The precise word for that position is contradictory. Workers in rich countries have a real interest in cheap imports and a real interest in the end of the arrangement that makes imports cheap, at the same time, permanently. That is not a fudge to avoid picking a side. It is the strongest claim the evidence actually supports, and the difference between it and the harder version matters enormously.

Why "contradictory" and not "Western workers are the enemy"

There is a maximal version of this argument: imperial superprofits have simply bought off the working class of the rich world, which is therefore a spent force, and revolutionary hope lies only in the Global South. That position has a name, third-worldism, and it fails on the evidence before you even get to the politics.

What the numbers show. Charles Post, "Exploring Working-Class Consciousness: A Critique of the Theory of the 'Labour-Aristocracy'", Historical Materialism 18(4), 2010, put the mechanism under measurement and found it could not carry that much weight. Foreign direct investment is a small share of total world investment, and most of it flows between rich countries rather than from poor to rich, so the pool of extracted wealth is not large enough to be buying off whole populations.

What the history shows. If superprofits pacify workers, the best-paid should be the quietest. They were the opposite. Miners, dockers and car workers, the most "aristocratic" sectors of Western labour, produced the most militant unions of the twentieth century, and dockers are still the group most likely to refuse to load a weapons shipment today.

What survives. Cheap imports, strong currencies and public services in the core were historically financed in part by conditions imposed elsewhere, and that much is hard to dispute. What it produces is divided interests, not purchased loyalty. And the practical difference is the whole point: divided interests can be organised, because one half of them points the same way as everyone else's. A bought-off class cannot be, which is why the maximal version ends by writing off the only large group of people you are actually in a position to organise alongside.

The point of saying this. Not guilt, which changes nothing and is mostly a way of feeling finished. Position. Guilt asks what kind of person you are. Class analysis asks where you are standing and what you can reach from there. Only the second one leads anywhere.

Station 2 of 5

How It Works: The Modern Mechanics

Extraction did not stop when the flags came down. It stopped needing soldiers. Debt repayments, loan conditions, trade rules and transfer pricing now move more wealth out of poor countries than colonial administrations ever did, and none of it requires an occupation.

Formal colonialism (flags, governors, gunboats) mostly ended by the 1970s.

Mechanism 1: Structural Adjustment. IMF and World Bank loans came with conditions: cut public spending, privatise, drop the tariffs protecting local industry. Across dozens of countries the result was consistent, local industry collapsed against subsidised Western competition, services were gutted, and the loans were repaid at interest. The promised development never arrived.

Deep Dive: The rigged cotton market

West African countries like Burkina Faso grow some of the world's highest-quality cotton at very low cost. Under structural adjustment, they were required to remove tariffs and subsidies for their farmers. Meanwhile, the US Farm Bill gave American cotton farmers $4 billion in annual subsidies, letting US cotton sell on world markets below its cost of production. West African farmers couldn't compete. Their industry collapsed. This is not a free market. It is a rigged market, and the rigging is documented in WTO filings.

Deep Dive: The wage hike the embassy killed

In June 2009, Haiti's Parliament voted unanimously to raise the minimum wage from about 24 cents an hour to 61 cents, roughly $5 a day. WikiLeaks cables released in 2011 by The Nation and Haïti Liberté show US Embassy and USAID officials meeting with contractors for Levi's, Hanes, and Fruit of the Loom, all of whom opposed the raise, and lobbying President René Préval directly. Préval carved out a separate, lower track for textile workers, about $3 a day, and it was his signature on the exemption. The embassy didn't need to act directly. It needed a comprador willing to enforce the outcome, and it had one.

The State Department's public rationale was competitiveness. Embassy cables argued that a wage that high would drive contractors to lower-wage competitors and threaten Haiti's garment-sector jobs, jobs that existed because of the HOPE Act, the US trade-preference law granting Haitian apparel duty-free access to the American market. That's a real economic argument, not an invented one, and it's the honest counter-case industry and US officials made. But PolitiFact, reviewing the same cables, rated the claim that the US blocked the raise as "strongly suggested but not fully proven": the documents show a coordinated meeting and direct pressure on Préval, not a signed order. No coup, no troops. Just cables, a trade law, and a president who complied.

Mechanism 2: Debt. Zambia spends more on debt service than on healthcare. Haiti paid France reparations for the "lost property" of slaveholders, the price of freeing itself from slavery, from 1825 until 1947, borrowing at interest to do it. 122 years.

Mechanism 3: Transfer Pricing. Multinationals move profits out before they can be taxed: sell Zambian copper to your own Swiss subsidiary cheap, sell it to the market at the real price, and the profit appears in Switzerland. The IMF estimates the cost to developing countries at $200 billion a year, more than they receive in aid.

Mechanism 4: The Growth Imperative. Capitalism requires infinite growth; when home markets saturate, capital looks outward. Cheap labour and weak regulation in the Global South aren't accidents, they're features capital actively seeks and defends.

Mechanism 5: The Militarized Anchor. None of this is enforceable by spreadsheet alone. Behind the loan conditions sits a carrier group: the US maintains roughly 750 military bases in 80 countries, guarding the chokepoints the money flows through.

Deep Dive: Why financial empire still needs gunboats

Financial imperialism is abstract. Supply chains are not. Oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz; 12% of global trade passes through the Suez Canal; undersea cables carry trillions through chokepoints with fixed geographic locations. These flows require physical security, which is why the US keeps its base network, and why it subsidizes allied military powers in regions where its own presence would be politically unsustainable.

A government that threatens to nationalise a resource or redirect a trade route must face credible force, not just unfavourable loan terms. Financial extraction and territorial military control are not a contradiction: one enforces the other. The Palestine guide (Part 3) examines the clearest case, a heavily subsidized settler state at the junction of three continents, adjacent to the world's largest oil reserves and its most critical shipping corridor.

Deep Dive: CIA Interventions

The Coups You Weren't Taught

One of the most documented but least discussed facts of 20th-century history is the systematic US policy of overthrowing democratically elected governments that threatened American corporate interests. This is not a conspiracy theory. These operations are now declassified, confirmed by the CIA itself, and in several cases formally apologised for by subsequent US administrations.

The pattern is consistent: a country elects a government that proposes nationalising a foreign-owned resource (oil, fruit, minerals), or land reform that would redistribute land held by US corporations. The US government characterises this as "communist." The CIA organises, funds, and in some cases directly executes a coup. A military dictatorship friendly to US business interests takes over. It typically tortures and murders the democratic opposition.

The CIA and British intelligence organised a coup. Mosaddegh was imprisoned. The Shah was installed. He ruled with SAVAK, his secret police, until the 1979 revolution, whose anti-American character the US intervention helped create. Confirmed and apologised for by the CIA in 2013.

Árbenz was replaced by a military dictatorship. A 36-year civil war followed in which 200,000 people were killed, mostly indigenous Maya. The Guatemalan genocide was committed by governments the US armed and trained.

Mobutu Sese Seko, a US-backed dictator, ruled for 32 years, stealing billions while the country collapsed. The Belgian government formally apologised in 2002.

The Nixon administration, working with the CIA, organised economic destabilisation and supported the military coup led by Augusto Pinochet. Allende died on the day of the coup. Pinochet's regime tortured and disappeared over 3,000 people. US involvement confirmed by declassified documents released in 1999.

The rest of the list

These are the most documented cases. The full list of confirmed or credibly alleged US interventions includes: Brazil (1964), Indonesia (1965, 500,000 killed), Greece (1967), Bolivia (multiple), Nicaragua (Contra war), Haiti (multiple), Venezuela (2002 attempt). Each followed similar patterns: elected government, resource threat to US interests, "communist" characterisation, destabilisation or coup.

The pattern: In almost every case, the governments targeted were not Stalinist dictatorships. They were social democrats, nationalists, and reformers who wanted their countries' resources to benefit their own people. The "communist" label was applied to any government that threatened US corporate access, regardless of whether it had any connection to the Soviet Union. Anticommunism was the justification. The real motive was the defence of profit.

This history matters for understanding the present. Many of today's migration crises trace directly to these interventions. The countries whose governments were overthrown didn't get to develop democratic institutions over decades. They got military dictatorships, civil wars, and debt. And then they were told their poverty was their own fault.

Deep Dive: The Debt System

How Poor Countries Pay Rich Ones

Here is a fact that inverts the conventional narrative about foreign aid: the Global South is a net creditor to the Global North. More money flows from poor countries to rich ones every year than flows in the reverse direction, including aid.

In the 1970s, oil-producing countries deposited enormous revenues in Western banks. Those banks needed to lend that money to earn returns. They found willing borrowers in newly independent developing countries, which needed capital to build infrastructure and industrialise. Loans were made at variable interest rates.

In 1979, the US Federal Reserve dramatically raised interest rates to combat domestic inflation. The effect on developing countries was catastrophic: their variable-rate dollar debt suddenly cost two to three times more to service. Countries across Latin America and Africa were instantly insolvent. They needed refinancing, which the IMF provided with structural adjustment conditions attached.

Haiti is the most extreme case. After the Haitian Revolution (1791 to 1804), the only successful slave revolt in history, France demanded reparations for the "lost property" of slaveholders: 150 million gold francs. Haiti, under threat of re-enslavement and blockade, agreed. It borrowed from French and American banks to pay. The final payment was made in 1947, 143 years after independence. Estimates suggest Haiti paid the equivalent of $21 billion in today's money for the crime of freeing itself.

Zambia today spends more on external debt service than on its entire public health system. It borrowed to build infrastructure; structural adjustment required opening its copper mines to foreign ownership; transfer pricing by mining multinationals means most copper profits leave the country before they can be taxed. The debt remains. The copper wealth is elsewhere.

The overall picture: According to Global Financial Integrity, developing countries lost $8.7 trillion to illicit financial flows between 2004 and 2013: capital flight, transfer pricing, and tax avoidance by multinationals operating in their territory. Over the same period, they received roughly $2 trillion in aid. They are lending the world $4 for every $1 they receive in charity.

The structural insight: The debt system isn't a series of unfortunate coincidences. It was built at Bretton Woods in 1944, explicitly designed by the US and UK to maintain dollar hegemony and Western financial dominance in the post-colonial era. Decolonisation changed the flags; Bretton Woods kept the money flowing in the same direction. Understanding this doesn't require malicious intent from every participant. It just requires following the incentive structures that were built in.
Deep Dive: The Democratic Republic of Congo

The Most Extracted Place on Earth

The Democratic Republic of Congo sits on an estimated $24 trillion in mineral wealth, the largest untapped mineral reserves of any country on earth. It contains 70% of the world's coltan (essential for every smartphone and laptop battery), vast copper and cobalt reserves, diamonds, gold, and uranium (the bomb dropped on Hiroshima used Congolese uranium).

The DRC is also one of the poorest countries on earth, with a GDP per capita of around $600 and life expectancy of 61 years. The relationship between these two facts is the story of the DRC.

Belgian colonialism (1885 to 1960) under King Leopold II is one of the most documented atrocities in colonial history. The Congo Free State was Leopold's personal property, not a Belgian colony but a private enterprise. Congolese people were forced to harvest wild rubber under a quota system. Leopold's colonial militia, the Force Publique, cut off the hands of workers and their children who fell short of the quota, sometimes killing them outright. Between 1885 and 1908, the Congolese population fell by an estimated 10 million people through murder, starvation, disease driven by forced labour, and flight. Leopold extracted an estimated $1.1 billion in today's money while this occurred.

After Belgian colonialism ended and Lumumba was assassinated (see the CIA deep dive), Mobutu Sese Seko, installed and supported by the US and Belgium, ruled for 32 years. He renamed the country Zaire, renamed himself, and stole an estimated $5 billion from state mining revenues, depositing it in Swiss banks while the country's infrastructure collapsed. He was supported by the US throughout, as an anticommunist ally, until the Cold War ended and he became inconvenient.

Today the DRC's eastern provinces have been in near-continuous armed conflict since 1996, a conflict that is a resource war, with multiple armed factions controlling mines and selling minerals through neighbouring countries to international supply chains. The phones, laptops, and electric vehicles whose batteries require Congolese cobalt and coltan reach consumers with no traceable connection to the conflict that extracts them.

The DRC is not poor because Congolese people lack initiative or the country lacks resources. For 140 years, every external actor with the power to shape its political economy has extracted from it rather than invested in it. The supply chain running through your phone is still doing it. What changed was the paperwork.

What the DRC tells us: Resource wealth without political sovereignty is not wealth. It is a liability that attracts extraction. The DRC's mineral abundance has caused its poverty. That is not in spite of the outside powers that have intervened in Congo for a century and a half. It is because of them. Understanding this requires no conspiracy: just the documented history of who controlled what, when, and where the money went.

The Record: What Western Powers Actually Did

Britain paid compensation for the abolition of slavery to the slaveholders, and British taxpayers finished paying off that loan in 2015. Not one penny went to a single enslaved person or their descendants. That is the register this record is written in.

A representative sample, chosen to calibrate scale. These are not allegations. They are documented history, most of it from Western sources.

1619–1865

Transatlantic Slave Trade

Britain paid compensation for abolition to the slaveholders, and finished paying it off in 2015 12.5 million people shipped. 2 million died in transit The enslaved and their descendants got nothing

The record

An estimated 12.5 million Africans were transported to the Americas as enslaved people; approximately 2 million died in transit. The labour of enslaved people built the agricultural wealth of the American South, the Caribbean sugar industry, and contributed substantially to British industrial capital. Reparations have never been paid to descendants. Britain only finished paying off the loan it took out to compensate slaveholders (not the enslaved) for abolition in 2015.

1757–1947

British Colonialism in India

Britain drained $45 trillion out of India India's share of world GDP: 25% in 1700, 4% by 1950 Bengal, 1943: 2–3 million starved while India exported food to Britain

The record

Economist Utsa Patnaik's research estimates Britain extracted $45 trillion from India between 1765 and 1938. India's share of global GDP fell from ~25% in 1700 to ~4% by 1950. The Bengal Famine of 1943 killed 2–3 million people while India exported food to Britain under colonial policy. British officials warned Winston Churchill the famine was killing millions; he responded by asking why Gandhi hadn't died yet.

1885–1960

The Scramble for Africa

Europe divided a continent between itself with not one African in the room Forced labour, taxation, institutions destroyed, resources shipped out

The record

European powers divided the African continent at the Berlin Conference without a single African representative present. Colonial administration used forced labour, imposed taxation (to force people into wage labour for colonial industries), destroyed existing political institutions, and extracted resources continuously. Belgium's Congo is the most extreme documented case but not unique in kind.

1945–1991

Cold War Interventions

The US changed the government of over 50 countries The USSR ran its own in Eastern Europe and Afghanistan Two northern powers fought it out using southern populations

The record

The US conducted or supported regime changes in over 50 countries during the Cold War, according to political scientist Lindsey O'Rourke's count in Covert Regime Change (2018). The Soviet Union conducted its own interventions in Eastern Europe, Afghanistan, and elsewhere. The Cold War was substantially fought in the Global South, using Global South populations as proxy combatants. Civil wars, dictatorships, and destroyed institutions were the consequences, and they outlasted the Cold War itself by decades.

1980s–2000s

Structural Adjustment

The countries that ignored the IMF did better than the ones that obeyed it 70+ countries took the loans and the conditions The UN found incomes fell and health and schooling decayed

The record

Over 70 countries underwent IMF and World Bank structural adjustment programmes. The UN's own assessment found that in Sub-Saharan Africa, structural adjustment produced declining incomes, rising inequality, and the deterioration of health and education systems in most countries that underwent it. The countries that ignored IMF advice, most notably South Korea, Taiwan, China, and Botswana, generally outperformed those that followed it.

A note on balance: Other empires were not benign. This guide focuses on Western imperialism because of its scale and ongoing structural consequences. The economic relationships it created are still running.

Station 4 of 5

The Propaganda: How This History Gets Hidden

None of this is classified. All of it is documented, most of it by the people who did it. It stays unknown not because it is hidden but because nothing in a national curriculum, a newsroom or a political vocabulary is built to carry it.

So why don't most people in wealthy countries know it? Not stupidity. Not a shadowy conspiracy. Structure.

The curriculum teaches history from inside the national story: the founding fathers, not the 1953 Iran coup, and not the Bengal Famine taught as a matter of policy. The language does political work invisibly: "development aid" sounds like generosity, "free trade" sounds neutral, "failed states" locates the failure inside the country. The incentives work quietly too: media owned by wealth and funded by corporate advertising doesn't aggressively platform structural critiques of wealth. The "communist" label did a century of work on its own: applying it to any government that threatened Western corporate access trained people to hear every critique of capital as Soviet apologia.

"The most effective propaganda is the kind you don't know you've received, because it arrived as the default assumption, the thing everyone just knows."

What this isn't saying: the West is not uniquely evil and the media are not uniformly false. The claim is narrower. The picture most people carry, poor countries poor from internal failings and rich countries rich on merit, is incomplete in ways that serve specific interests.

Deep Dive: How Media Frames Global South Poverty

The Story That Gets Told, and the One That Doesn't

The academic field of media studies has produced substantial research on how Western media covers the Global South. The patterns are consistent enough to be treated as structural rather than accidental.

The depoliticisation of poverty. When Western media covers poverty in Africa or Latin America, it most commonly frames it as a humanitarian crisis requiring charitable response: images of suffering, appeals to viewers' generosity, donations to NGOs. This framing is not false, but it is incomplete: it presents poverty as a natural disaster or a medical condition rather than as a political outcome with specific causes and beneficiaries. It generates sympathy without generating understanding.

The corruption frame. Stories about developing country governments focus heavily on corruption: leaders stealing public funds, embezzlement, mismanagement. Corruption in developing countries is real and damaging. But this frame consistently omits context: the multinational corporations that pay the bribes, the Western banks that accept the stolen funds (Switzerland, Luxembourg, and the UK's overseas territories are the world's largest tax havens), and the structural conditions (low public sector wages, weak institutional capacity) that colonialism created and structural adjustment maintained.

The charity vs. justice frame. Development coverage almost universally uses the language of charity: rich countries "give" aid to poor ones. This frame positions wealthy countries as generous donors and poor countries as grateful recipients. The alternative frame, justice, reparations, the return of extracted wealth, rarely appears, because it would recast the relationship from generosity to obligation. The charity frame requires no structural change; the justice frame does.

A concrete comparison: When a Western bank is implicated in laundering money from a developing country dictator, as happened repeatedly with Deutsche Bank, HSBC, and others, the story is typically framed as a banking scandal. The structural question, why do Western financial systems make it so easy to extract and hide wealth from poor countries, gets less coverage. When a developing country government is corrupt, it is a story about that country. When a Western bank enables it, it is a story about the bank. The country's poverty remains unexplained.

The solution: reading widely and sourcing differently. None of this means mainstream sources are useless. It means they should be read alongside sources that don't share their structural incentives: academic development economics (Jason Hickel, Utsa Patnaik, Ha-Joon Chang), investigative journalism focused on financial flows (Global Financial Integrity, Tax Justice Network), and writers from the Global South covering their own countries' political economies (not just Western correspondents parachuted in).

Now What?

The uncomfortable part is not that this was done. It's that you are standing inside the result. If you live in a wealthy country, the extraction did not happen somewhere else to someone else. It ended up here, in the prices you pay and the services you use.

A clear view of this history doesn't point to one political conclusion. The live positions:

Reparations: the extraction was large enough, recent enough, and calculable enough that direct transfers are owed. Caricom, the political and economic union of fifteen Caribbean nations, has pursued a formal claim against European powers since 2013, framed around debt cancellation, health systems and development programmes rather than a lump sum, which is a direct response to the objection that matters most. The objections about who pays and how much are real. So is the one the deep dive off Station 1 raises: a transfer to a state is a transfer to whoever runs that state, and extraction had partners at both ends.

Structural reform: forget adjudicating history; fix the current mechanisms. Close tax havens, restructure IMF conditionality, cancel odious debt, let developing countries protect emerging industries the way rich countries did when they were developing.

Solidarity rather than shopping: the extraction ends in your pocket. Congolese cobalt in your phone, structural-adjustment wages in your clothes. The earlier version of this guide listed consumer choice here, which was a mistake, and one this series had already refuted: Part 1's whole argument is that structural problems do not have consumer solutions, and no purchasing decision has ever reached the IMF. What has moved these mechanisms is organised pressure in the countries doing the extracting. The Jubilee 2000 coalition, campaigning inside creditor countries, forced the HIPC and MDRI debt-relief programmes, the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries Initiative and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative, which have since cancelled over $100 billion of debt across 37 countries, 31 of them in Africa (Source: World Bank, HIPC Initiative). The arms trade gets disrupted by dockworkers refusing to load ships, not by shoppers. The leverage a person in a wealthy country actually holds runs through their workplace, their union and their government, not their basket.

The hardest part: none of it produces clean moral categories. Nobody alive chose this history, not in the countries built on extracted wealth and not in the countries it was extracted from. But not choosing it is different from not standing in it. What justice looks like across that gap is one of the hard questions of the century, and it can't be answered without first knowing what happened.

"There are no poor nations. There are only nations whose wealth has been stolen, and nations doing the stealing."
Paraphrasing Walter Rodney, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, 1972

Rodney's own book is more precise than this paraphrase, and the difference matters. He wrote about classes, not only nations: European capital did the taking, and African ruling groups took a commission on it. He was assassinated in Guyana in 1980, aged 38, organising against a government of Black Guyanese. Read the sentence above as a summary of where the wealth went, not of who was standing where.

That's the guide. What you have is a framework, not a verdict. Where you take it is yours to decide.
